Analyst Revisions, News Tone and Slow Information Diffusion in the Cross-Section of Stock Returns

(Sample note written for testing paper2alpha. It summarises well-known findings from the empirical
asset-pricing literature in plain words; it is not a reproduction of any specific paper.)

Abstract
Investors under-react to new information about firm fundamentals. We study two public signals that
arrive gradually: revisions of sell-side analysts' earnings forecasts, and the tone of news stories
about a firm. Stocks whose consensus earnings forecasts were revised upward over the previous month
earn higher returns over the following one to six months than stocks whose forecasts were revised
downward. The effect is stronger when revisions are broad-based (many analysts move in the same
direction) and when forecast dispersion is low. Negative news tone predicts low returns over the next
days and weeks, and the reaction is slower for stories with high novelty and relevance to the firm.

1. Introduction
Analysts update forecasts in small steps, and prices incorporate their revisions with a delay. A
revision measured over 30 days captures this drift. Counting how many analysts raise versus lower
their forecasts gives a breadth measure that is less noisy than the average revision itself.
Earnings surprises, the difference between reported and expected earnings, also lead to drift
after the announcement.

2. Data and signals
Revision signal: the average percentage change of the consensus EPS forecast for the current fiscal
year over the past 30 days, cross-sectionally ranked each day.
Breadth signal: (number of upward revisions - number of downward revisions) / number of estimates.
Dispersion: the standard deviation of analyst forecasts scaled by the absolute mean forecast;
high dispersion weakens the revision signal.
News tone: the average sentiment score of news stories about the firm on each day, weighted by the
story's relevance to the firm; days without news carry no signal.

3. Results
Sorting stocks into deciles on the 30-day revision signal, the top-minus-bottom portfolio earns a
monthly return spread of roughly 0.8 percent, with most of the effect in the first three months.
Combining revisions with breadth improves the Sharpe ratio, and conditioning on low dispersion
improves it further. Within industries the effect remains, which suggests it is not an industry
momentum effect. News tone predicts returns over the next one to five days; averaging tone over
two weeks lowers turnover with modest loss of predictive power. Novel stories have larger and
longer-lasting effects than repeated stories.

4. Robustness
Results hold in large and liquid stocks, survive industry neutralisation, and are not explained by
price momentum. Returns are lower after transaction costs for the daily news signal because of its
high turnover; smoothing the signal over time recovers most of the after-cost performance.

5. Conclusion
Gradual diffusion of analyst information and news tone produces predictable cross-sectional
returns. Breadth and low disagreement amplify revision signals; novelty amplifies news signals.
